# MUSTER — a readiness system for local service

*(merge of Reps + Muster: the logistics layer plus the habit-and-reward layer that keeps people coming back)*

Group theme: Tech as a Tool

---

## One-sentence version

Muster turns a town's unmet needs into taskable units, matches them to the capabilities its
residents actually have, and then rewards the one thing civic life never measures: **showing up
when you said you would, week after week.**

## The problem, stated precisely

Civic volunteering does not fail on goodwill. It fails on three things:

1. **Supply chain.** Demand is unstructured ("we need help"), capacity is invisible (nobody knows
   who owns a welder, a truck, or fluent Spanish), and matching is done by whoever happens to be
   on a group text.
2. **Loop closure.** Nobody reports the outcome, so effort disappears into a void and people
   don't return.
3. **Cadence.** Service is treated as episodic and heroic — the campaign, the crisis, the annual
   volunteer day. We have excellent technology for building physical habits and essentially none
   for civic ones.

Muster fixes 1 and 2 with logistics doctrine, and 3 with a reward system built around
reliability rather than heroism.

## How it works

**Needs in.** A need arrives from anyone — a neighbor, a church, a school, the county. Muster
decomposes it into taskable units, each with time, skill, and materiel requirements. "Help the
Hansens after the flood" becomes eleven tasks, three of which need a truck and one of which needs
someone who can operate a pump.

**Capability registry.** Residents register what they actually have: skills, equipment, licenses,
languages, and honest availability windows. Capability, not intention — the registry knows the
town owns four generators and one bilingual paramedic.

**The weekly rep.** Every member holds a standing commitment: one small unit per week, default
20 minutes, at a fixed time, with a named partner. The system pre-scopes it so there is nothing
to decide and nothing to organize. Miss a week and it does not guilt you; it *shrinks the ask* —
miss two and it offers a five-minute rep to keep the chain alive.

**Surge vs. sustainment.** Two modes, borrowed straight from readiness doctrine. Sustainment is
the weekly rep. Surge is the flood, the fire, the funeral — and the whole point of drilling weekly
is that when a surge comes, the town already knows who has a truck and who answers their phone.

**After-action report.** Every completed need gets a short public AAR: what was needed, who
turned out, what it took, what we'd do differently. Effort becomes visible and legible, which is
what actually produces the next turnout.

## The reward layer (the merge)

Design rule: **reward reliability, not volume, and never let the reward become the reason.** The
currency is trust, and it is earned by keeping small promises.

- **Readiness rating.** Your personal standing is your *show-rate* — commitments kept over
  commitments made. Not hours logged, not tasks completed. Volunteering 40 hours once and then
  vanishing is worth less than 20 minutes a week for two years, and Muster is the first system
  that says so out loud.
- **Squads.** You join a small unit of 4–8 neighbors. Streaks are held by the squad, not the
  individual — so the person having a hard month is carried by their squad instead of shamed by a
  leaderboard, and the social pressure is the good kind (people you know are counting on you).
  Public rankings are squad-level only.
- **Quals.** Qualifications, in the military sense, rather than badges: chainsaw, pump operator,
  Spanish interpreter, wildfire prep, elder check-in, meeting minutes. Earned by demonstration,
  visible in the registry, and *actually consequential* — quals determine what you get called for
  in a surge. A qual is a credential, not a sticker.
- **Standing.** Sustained readiness unlocks real standing: sponsorship rights to put a need on
  the board, a vote on the quarterly task menu, the ability to stand up your own squad. Earned
  authority over the system, which is more motivating than points and is also just good
  governance.
- **Local reciprocity.** Businesses can honor readiness however they choose — coffee, a discount,
  reserved seats at the game. Kept small and local on purpose: the reward is recognition by your
  own town, not a gift card economy.
- **The wall.** A physical board somewhere public — the library, the hardware store, the diner —
  showing this month's needs met, the squads that met them, and what's still open. The digital
  system exists to feed the wall.

**Anti-gaming, said honestly in the presentation.** Any metric becomes a target. Muster's
defenses: show-rate can't be farmed because commitments are capped at one rep a week; tasks are
verified by the *requester*, not self-reported; rankings are squad-level so individual
glory-seeking has nowhere to go; and rewards stay symbolic and local so no one has a financial
reason to game them. If we get this wrong, we crowd out the intrinsic motive and the whole thing
dies — so the reward layer stays deliberately thin.

## What "good" looks like

- **Show-rate** across the community (commitments kept / commitments made)
- **Median weeks of unbroken participation**, and retention at 6 and 12 months — the number no
  volunteer program publishes
- **Time from need posted to need met**
- **Percent of registered capacity actually used** (an idle welder is a supply failure, not an
  apathy problem)
- **Surge readiness:** how fast the town fielded people the last time something went wrong

## The line to close on

We already know how to make a body strong and how to make a unit ready. We have never applied
either to citizenship. Muster is the claim that a healthier public culture is less a matter of
persuasion than of **reps, logistics, and keeping small promises to people you can see.**
